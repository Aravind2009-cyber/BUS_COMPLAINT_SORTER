# Capstone Reflection: Engineering AI-Native Safety Systems
**Author:** AI-Native Engineering Student · CIT Semester 1 · Assessment 5  
**Word Count:** 415 words

Building the Bus Complaint Sorter fundamentally altered my perspective on building AI-native engineering systems. At the start of the semester, I viewed LLMs as all-in-one computational engines capable of ingesting raw text and directly solving end-to-end problems. However, developing this capstone taught me that production-grade AI systems succeed not because of the generative power of LLMs alone, but because of the deterministic software scaffolding built around them.

The most critical engineering lesson occurred during the design of route extraction. An early naive implementation asked the LLM to extract the route and group complaints per route. The LLM repeatedly hallucinated: it classified clock times like "7:00 AM" as Route 7, miscounted complaint totals, and occasionally hallucinated non-existent routes like Route 15. This failure highlighted the division of responsibilities: probabilistic models excel at semantic interpretation—understanding that a student writing *"Driver anna romba fast ah poraru"* describes a dangerous driver—while deterministic Python code excels at arithmetic, counting, and rigid constraint verification. Enforcing the rule that Python must deterministically verify the route against `routes.csv` and regex-match it against the raw complaint string transformed an unreliable prototype into a robust pipeline.

Furthermore, formulating the Judgment Framework clarified the ethical boundaries of AI engineering. In high-stakes domains like campus transit, the cost of a false negative is asymmetric. If the system fails to flag a broken rear door or worn-out brakes, students risk physical injury. Conversely, the cost of a false positive is merely 30 seconds of an administrator’s time. This asymmetry drove the prompt rubric: every complaint involving physical vehicle defects or reckless driving is aggressively elevated to the Transport Officer's safety queue. Crucially, the tool never acts autonomously—it never contacts drivers or assigns disciplinary fault. The AI acts exclusively as an intake triage sieve, preserving the human officer as the sole accountable decision-maker.

Finally, designing the resilient fallback mechanism reinforced the golden rule of reliability: an unhandled exception or malformed JSON should never crash a batch pipeline halfway. By wrapping LLM invocations in a retry loop and providing an explicit fallback state (`needs_manual_review`), the tool guarantees that no student complaint is dropped silently. Ultimately, this capstone demonstrated that responsible AI engineering is the discipline of wrapping probabilistic models inside deterministic checks, robust error handling, and human-in-the-loop governance.
